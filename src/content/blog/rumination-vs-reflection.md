Something went wrong today. A comment at work, a sharp text, a mistake you can't take back.

You told yourself you would think it through properly.

That was two hours ago. You are still on the same question. Why did I say that? Why does this always happen to me?

You don't feel any wiser. You feel worse.

---

## You are not alone in this

Almost everyone goes over painful things in their head. It is how we try to make sense of them. Most of the time it settles on its own.

Sometimes it doesn't. Psychologists call the stuck version rumination: thinking again and again about how bad you feel, and why, without getting anywhere.

It matters because of what it predicts. In a 2008 review in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, Blair Wisco and Sonja Lyubomirsky brought together two decades of research. One study they describe followed a community sample of around 1,300 adults. People who tended to ruminate were more likely to go on to have a new episode of major depression.

So a looping mind is very ordinary. But it is worth learning to spot.

---

## Two kinds of thinking that look the same

From the inside, rumination and reflection feel identical. Both are you, alone, thinking hard about something that hurt.

In 2003, Wendy Treynor, Richard Gonzalez and Nolen-Hoeksema took apart the questionnaire used to measure rumination. They found it held two different things.

**Brooding.** Dwelling on how things should be different. Asking "why me?" Comparing where you are with where you think you ought to be.

**Reflection**, or reflective pondering. Turning inward to try to understand something, more like going somewhere quiet to think about your feelings.

The difference showed up over time. Brooding went with more depression at the time and a year later. Reflection also went with more low mood at the time. But over the following year it was linked with less depression, not more. The researchers suggested reflection may be uncomfortable in the short run but helpful in the long run.

That is a useful thing to know. Feeling bad while you think something through is not, by itself, a sign you are doing it wrong.

## Why "why" can trap you

Ed Watkins, a psychologist at the University of Exeter, has spent years on this question. In a 2008 review in *Psychological Bulletin*, he argued that repetitive thought can be constructive or unconstructive. One of the things that decides which is how abstract the thinking is.

Abstract thinking asks why. Why did this happen? What does it say about me? What does it mean for my future?

Concrete thinking asks how. What exactly happened, moment by moment? What did I feel, and when? What is the next thing I could do?

When the subject is something painful, Watkins found that the abstract style tends to go with worse outcomes. In studies he reviews, people who dwelt on a failure in an abstract way stayed in a worse mood for longer than those who thought about it in a concrete way.

"Why am I like this?" has no end point. Every answer opens another question. "What is one thing I could do tomorrow?" does have an end point.

---

## Why the usual advice fails

The most common advice is to stop thinking about it. That rarely works for long. Telling yourself not to think about something keeps it in view.

The second most common advice is to keep thinking until you understand. That is often what turned reflection into brooding in the first place.

Distraction sits somewhere in between. In the 2008 review, lab studies showed that a short, absorbing distraction could lift low mood and help people solve problems more clearly afterwards. But the authors also warned that using distraction all the time, without ever coming back to the problem, can slide into avoidance.

So the goal is not to stop thinking. It is to change how you think.

---

## What helps

**Notice the question you are asking.** If it starts with "why" and you have asked it ten times, you are probably brooding. Try swapping it for a "how" or "what next" question.

**Get concrete.** Go back to what actually happened, in order, in plain detail. What was said. What you felt. What you did. This is often less painful than the big story about what it all means.

**Step back.** In 2005, Ethan Kross, Ozlem Ayduk and Walter Mischel ran two studies with students in *Psychological Science*. Each person recalled a time they had felt very angry with someone. Some were told to relive it as if they were back there. Others were told to take a few steps back and watch the scene as if it were happening to a more distant version of themselves.

Both groups were asked to think about why they felt as they did. The people who stepped back felt less anger and less negative emotion afterwards. When they described the event, they retold the blow by blow less, and made more sense of it. In other words, the same "why" question helped when asked from a distance.

**Give it a time and a limit.** If something keeps coming back, decide when you will think about it properly, for how long, and with a pen. Then do that, and stop.

**Do something absorbing.** A walk, a call with someone, a task with your hands. Used as a break rather than a hiding place.

There is also a specific therapy built on these ideas. Rumination-focused CBT was developed by Watkins and colleagues. In a small 2011 trial in the *British Journal of Psychiatry*, 42 people with depression that had not fully lifted on medication were given either usual care or usual care plus up to 12 sessions of this therapy. The added therapy led to fewer remaining symptoms and more people in remission, and the change seemed to work through reduced rumination. It was a small, early trial with no comparison therapy, so it is a promising start rather than the last word.

## Where this fits in Fullfill

This is the part of our 4C method we call Clarity. The other three are Calmness, Control and Connection.

I think Clarity is often misunderstood. It is not having every answer. It is being able to tell when your thinking is getting you somewhere, and when it has started going round in circles.

That is hard to learn in the middle of a spiral. It is much easier to practise on an ordinary day, with something small. Train the mind while it's calm, and the skill is more likely to be there when you need it.

## When to get more help

Rumination is closely tied to depression and anxiety. If it is going on most days, if your mood has been low for more than a couple of weeks, or if it is getting in the way of work, sleep or relationships, please speak to your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, without going through your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
