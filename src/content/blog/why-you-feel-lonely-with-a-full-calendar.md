Your week was full. Meetings, a team lunch, a group chat that never goes quiet.

On Friday night you sit down and realise you haven't had a real conversation in days.

There are people you could text. You scroll past their names and put the phone down.

It feels silly to call it loneliness. You are hardly ever alone.

---

## You are not the only one

Loneliness is not the same as being alone. Researchers define it as the gap between the connection you have and the connection you want. You can be surrounded by people and still feel that gap.

It is common. In the government's Community Life Survey for 2024/25, 7% of adults in England said they felt lonely often or always. The highest figure was among 16 to 24 year olds, at 9%. Those are only the people who said "often or always". Many more feel it some of the time.

It also matters for health. In 2010, Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy Smith and Bradley Layton pooled 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people in *PLoS Medicine*. People with stronger social relationships had a 50% higher likelihood of survival over the study periods. That is an association, not proof that friends make you live longer, but it is a large and consistent one.

So if you feel this, you are not being dramatic. It is one of the most ordinary human experiences there is.

---

## What loneliness does to your thinking

The late John Cacioppo spent much of his career at the University of Chicago studying loneliness. With Louise Hawkley, he set out a model in a 2009 paper in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*.

Their argument goes like this. For most of human history, being cut off from the group was dangerous. So when we feel isolated, the brain shifts into a more watchful mode. It becomes more alert to social threat. Rejection looks more likely. Neutral faces look a bit colder. You expect less from people.

That watchfulness then changes how you act. You hold back. You wait for others to reach out first. You read a slow reply as a sign. Other people pick up on the distance, and the gap gets a little wider.

None of this is a character flaw. It is a protective system doing its job a bit too well.

## You are probably more liked than you think

Two lines of research show how far off our predictions can be.

In 2014, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked commuters on trains and buses in Chicago to do one of three things: talk to a stranger, keep to themselves, or travel as normal. They published the results in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*. People who talked had a more pleasant journey than those who sat in silence. Yet a separate group, asked to imagine the same thing, predicted the opposite. Commuters expected more than half of strangers to brush them off. In the studies where people were asked to start a chat, none reported being rebuffed.

In 2018, Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark described what they called the liking gap, in *Psychological Science*. After a first conversation, people consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them. They missed the signs of interest and enjoyment that were there. In one study of university roommates, the gap lasted for months.

Put these together with Cacioppo's model and you can see the trap. Loneliness makes you expect less warmth. And even without loneliness, most of us already expect less warmth than we get.

## Why the usual advice fails

The usual advice is to get out more. Join a club. Say yes to more invitations.

If your diary is already full, this misses the point. Being around people is not the same as feeling known by them. A busy week of meetings and group plans can leave the gap exactly where it was.

There is a second problem. If your mind is braced for rejection, more contact can simply feed it. You go to the party, notice everyone who didn't talk to you, and leave more convinced than before.

Christopher Masi, Hsi Yuan Chen, Louise Hawkley and John Cacioppo tested this question directly. In a 2011 meta-analysis in *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, they looked at 50 studies of interventions to reduce loneliness. They sorted them into four kinds: building social skills, adding social support, creating more chances to meet people, and working on the thoughts people have about others. Among the randomised trials, the approaches that worked best were the ones that tackled those unhelpful thoughts. More opportunity on its own did less.

## What helps

**Notice the forecast.** Before you message someone, you probably predict how it will land. "They're busy." "It'll be awkward." Treat that as a forecast, not a fact. The research above suggests it is likely to be too gloomy.

**Go for depth, not volume.** One honest conversation does more for the gap than five polite ones. Ask a question you actually want the answer to. Say something true about your week.

**Give it time.** Friendship takes longer than most of us allow. In a 2019 study in the *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, Jeffrey Hall at the University of Kansas surveyed adults who had recently moved, and followed new university students. He estimated it took around 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, around 90 hours to become friends, and more than 200 hours to become close friends. Hours spent working side by side counted for much less than time spent hanging out. A few coffees and then giving up is not a fair test.

**Keep small contact going.** A short chat with the barista or a neighbour won't replace a close friend. But the commuter studies suggest it lifts mood more than we expect, and it keeps the social muscle in use.

**Reach out first, and keep it simple.** "Thought of you today, how are things?" is enough. Most people are glad to hear from someone. Few of us send that message, because we all assume the other person is less keen than we are.

## When to get more help

Loneliness is not a mental health condition in itself. But long stretches of it are closely linked with low mood and anxiety. If you have felt low or cut off most days for more than a couple of weeks, or it is affecting your sleep, work or appetite, please speak to your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies without going through your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
