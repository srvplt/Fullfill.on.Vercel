You have something to say in the meeting. You have had it ready for ten minutes.

You wait for a gap. Then for a better gap. Then someone else says something close to it, and the moment goes.

On the way home you tell yourself you will speak up when you feel a bit more confident.

Next week, the same thing happens.

---

## Most people are waiting for the same feeling

Almost everyone has a version of this. The phone call you keep putting off. The question you don't ask. The gym you would go to once you felt fitter.

The idea underneath is reasonable. Confident people act, so first I need to become confident, and then I will act.

The research suggests the order is mostly the other way round.

Confidence and doing well certainly go together. In 1998, Alexander Stajkovic and Fred Luthans pooled 114 studies with more than 21,000 people in *Psychological Bulletin*. They found a clear positive link between self-efficacy and work performance. But a link between two things doesn't tell you which one comes first.

In 2013, Traci Sitzmann and Gillian Yeo pulled together studies that tracked the same people over time, measuring their confidence in a task and how well they then did it. They published the results in *Personnel Psychology*. Once they allowed for people simply improving with practice, confidence did very little to predict how well someone did next time. How well they had just done was a much stronger predictor of how confident they then felt. Their conclusion was that self-efficacy is mainly a product of past performance.

In other words, confidence tends to show up after you have done the thing. Not before.

---

## What confidence actually is

The psychologist Albert Bandura gave this feeling a more precise name. In 1977 he published a paper in *Psychological Review* called "Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change".

Self-efficacy is not a general sense of self-belief. It is your judgement about whether you can do a particular thing. Speaking in this meeting. Making this call. You can have high self-efficacy for driving and very low self-efficacy for public speaking, both at once.

Bandura argued that these beliefs matter because they shape whether you start something, how much effort you put in, and how long you keep going when it gets hard.

He also set out where these beliefs come from. There are four main sources:

- **Doing it yourself.** He called these performance accomplishments, or mastery experiences.
- **Watching people like you do it.**
- **Being told you can.**
- **How your body feels.** A racing heart gets read as "I can't", even when it is just arousal.

He was clear that the first one counts most. Mastery experience, he wrote, is especially influential because it is based on your own experience of succeeding. Being told you can do something tends to produce a weaker belief, and on its own it has limits as a way of building lasting confidence.

That one point explains a lot about why confidence advice so often doesn't stick.

---

## Why the usual advice fails

**Pep talks.** "You've got this" is verbal persuasion, Bandura's weakest source. It can get you through a door. It rarely changes what you believe about yourself, because part of you knows it is just words.

**Waiting until you feel ready.** If confidence mostly comes from what you have done, then waiting to feel confident means waiting for something that needs action to arrive. The longer you wait, the less evidence you collect, and the bigger the task starts to look.

**Doing it, but with a safety net.** This one is sneaky. You do speak up, but you read from notes word for word, avoid eye contact and leave quickly. In 1995, Adrian Wells, David Clark and colleagues at Oxford tested this with eight people with social phobia, in *Behavior Therapy*. Each did one session of facing a feared situation as normal, and one where they also dropped their usual safety behaviours. Dropping them led to a bigger fall in anxiety and in belief that the feared disaster would happen. The idea is that safety behaviours stop you learning that you would have been fine anyway. You get through it, but you credit the safety net, not yourself.

**Aiming too high.** Jumping straight to the hardest version and then struggling can teach the opposite lesson. A failure early on is still evidence, just the wrong kind.

---

## What helps

The common thread in the evidence is simple. Collect small, real experiences of doing the thing, and let your confidence catch up.

**Shrink the step.** Pick a version of the task you are fairly sure you can do. Not "speak up more in meetings", but "ask one question in Tuesday's meeting". Success is what builds the belief, so set things up to succeed.

**Climb in order.** This is the basic shape of graded exposure, used in CBT for anxiety. You list situations from easiest to hardest and work up, staying with each until it feels more manageable. Bandura's 1977 paper described the same process: repeatedly doing things that feel threatening but are actually safe builds a sense of mastery, which raises confidence further.

**Drop one safety behaviour at a time.** If you always rehearse every sentence, try one conversation without a script. If you always hold a drink as a prop, put it down. Then notice what actually happened.

**Write down what happened, not what you feared.** Memory is unkind to small wins. A one-line note after each step ("asked the question, two people nodded, nobody looked at me oddly") gives you a record your nerves can't easily argue with.

**Watch people like you.** Bandura's second source is seeing someone similar to you manage it. A colleague at your level is more useful here than a polished TED speaker.

**Expect the nerves to come along.** A faster heart before you speak is not a sign you aren't ready. It is your body getting ready. If that physical side tends to run away with you, I wrote about what is happening in the body in [this post on panic](/blog/why-panic-feels-like-dying).

## When to get more help

If fear of being judged is stopping you from going to work, seeing people or doing things you need to do, or if you have felt anxious or low for more than a few weeks, please speak to your GP. Social anxiety is common and it is treatable. NICE recommends a form of CBT developed specifically for social anxiety as the first treatment for adults. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, without seeing your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
