You are about to walk into a hard conversation. Or you have just read an email you wish you hadn't.

Your shoulders are up near your ears. Your chest feels tight. Your breathing is quick and high, and you hadn't noticed until now.

Someone tells you to take a deep breath. So you do. You haul in a big lungful of air.

It doesn't help much. If anything, you feel a bit more wound up.

---

## Breathing is one of the few levers you can reach

Most of what your body does under stress happens without asking you. You can't tell your heart to slow down or your stomach to unknot.

Breathing is different. It runs on autopilot, but you can take the controls whenever you like.

In 2023, Guy Fincham and colleagues pooled 12 randomised controlled trials with 785 adults in *Scientific Reports*. People who did breathwork reported lower stress than people in control groups. The effect was small to medium in size. They found similar small effects for anxiety and low mood.

The authors were careful. Most of the trials had a moderate risk of bias, the total sample was small, and they warned against reading too much into it. Breathing exercises seem to help some people, some of the time. That is a modest claim, but it is a real one.

---

## What slow breathing does in your body

Your heart rate is not fixed from beat to beat. It rises a little when you breathe in and falls a little when you breathe out. This is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and it is a normal sign of a healthy system.

The out-breath is linked to the vagus nerve, a major part of the branch of your nervous system that settles you down after stress. So a long, slow exhale nudges your heart rate down, and your body reads that as a signal that things are safe.

In 2018, Andrea Zaccaro and colleagues reviewed 15 studies of slow breathing in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*. They defined slow breathing as 10 breaths a minute or fewer. Across the studies, slower breathing tended to increase heart rate variability, which the authors read as more activity in that calming branch. Some studies also reported people feeling more relaxed and less anxious.

That review has limits. None of the studies were randomised controlled trials, most were small, and several found no change in how people felt.

## The study that put the sigh on the map

The study most people mean when they talk about the "physiological sigh" was published in January 2023 in *Cell Reports Medicine* by Melis Yilmaz Balban, David Spiegel, Andrew Huberman and colleagues at Stanford.

They randomly assigned 108 people, mostly students, to one of four practices. Each was done for five minutes a day, at home, for a month.

- **Cyclic sighing.** Breathe in slowly, then take a second, shorter breath in to fill the lungs fully. Then breathe out slowly, as far as is comfortable.
- **Box breathing.** Breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold, each for the same count.
- **Cyclic hyperventilation.** Rounds of fast, deep breaths followed by a short hold with empty lungs.
- **Mindfulness meditation.** Sitting quietly with attention on one spot, without changing the breath.

Every group felt less anxious after their daily session. On anxiety and negative mood, the breathing groups did no better than meditation.

The difference showed up in positive mood. The breathing groups as a whole gained more than the meditation group, and cyclic sighing was the only single practice that was clearly ahead. It also produced the biggest drop in resting breathing rate over the month. People whose breathing rate fell more tended to report bigger lifts in mood.

I think this study is interesting, not conclusive. It was a small, exploratory trial, registered after it had started. It was done remotely, so nobody could check how carefully people practised. It is a good reason to try cyclic sighing. It is not proof that it beats everything else.

## Why "take a deep breath" often fails

The usual advice puts the weight on the wrong half of the breath.

When people are told to take a deep breath, most of them gulp a large one in, often through the mouth, often up in the chest. The in-breath is the part that nudges your heart rate up. And breathing big and fast when you are already tense can tip you towards the light-headed, tingly feelings that come from blowing off too much carbon dioxide.

The research above points the other way. The common thread is a slower rate and a longer, unhurried exhale. In the Stanford study, the practice that emphasised the out-breath did best, and the fast breathing practice was not clearly better than sitting quietly.

The other problem is timing. Most of us only try to breathe calmly when we are already stressed, which is the hardest moment to learn anything new.

## What helps

**Make the out-breath longer than the in-breath.** You don't need a precise count. Breathe in gently through your nose, then let the breath out slowly through your mouth, taking longer to empty than you took to fill.

**Try a few cyclic sighs.** A full breath in through the nose, a short top-up breath in, then a long, slow breath out. One to three of these can be enough in a tense moment. For practice, the Stanford participants did it for five minutes.

**Go slower, not bigger.** Aim to bring your breathing towards the slower end, around six to ten breaths a minute, without forcing it. If you feel dizzy, breathe more gently and stop for a moment.

**Practise when you don't need it.** Five minutes a day, when nothing is wrong, makes the pattern familiar.

**Keep your expectations honest.** Breathing can take the edge off. It won't fix whatever is causing the stress, and it isn't a treatment for an anxiety disorder.

## When to get more help

If stress or anxiety has been with you most days for a few weeks, if it is affecting your sleep, work or relationships, or if you are avoiding things because of it, please speak to your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for CBT and other psychological support, without seeing your GP first.

If you have a heart or lung condition, or breathing exercises make you feel unwell, check with your GP before practising regularly.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
