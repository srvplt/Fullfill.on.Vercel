It usually starts with something small.

A skipped heartbeat in a meeting. A tight chest on a crowded train. A wave of dizziness in the supermarket queue.

Then, within a minute or two, it is everything. Your heart is pounding. You can't get a full breath. Your hands tingle. And one thought takes over: something is very wrong with me.

Many people who have a panic attack for the first time go to A&E. They are sure it is their heart. The tests come back normal. They are sent home relieved, and a little embarrassed.

And then they start waiting for the next one.

---

## You are not as unusual as it feels

Panic attacks are common. A study of more than 140,000 adults across 25 countries, published in 2016 as part of the World Mental Health Surveys, found that about 13% of people have at least one panic attack in their lifetime.

Most of them never go on to develop panic disorder. For many, it happens once or twice, during a stressful stretch of life, and fades.

So if it has happened to you, you are in very large company. It does not mean you are weak or breaking. It means your alarm system fired.

---

## What is actually happening in your body

A panic attack is your fight or flight response going off at full volume, with no lion in the room.

Adrenaline rises. Your heart speeds up to push blood to your muscles. Your breathing gets faster and shallower. That fast breathing lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood, which is what causes the tingling, the light head and the strange, unreal feeling many people describe.

Every one of those sensations is a sign of a system working hard. Not a system failing.

The psychiatric definition is useful here. A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes. Peaks. It rises, and then the body brings it back down. Adrenaline cannot keep climbing forever.

Always get new or unexplained chest pain checked by a doctor. But once that has been done, it helps to know what you are dealing with.

## The loop that keeps it going

In 1986, the Oxford psychologist David Clark published a model of panic that is still the backbone of treatment today.

His idea was simple. Panic is not caused by the sensations. It is caused by what you decide the sensations mean.

It goes like this:

- You notice something in your body. A flutter, a tight breath.
- You read it as danger. "Heart attack." "I'm going to faint." "I'm losing it."
- That thought makes you more afraid.
- Fear makes the sensations stronger.
- Stronger sensations seem to prove you were right.

Round and round, faster each time. That is why panic feels like it comes from nowhere. The trigger is often a normal body sensation you would barely notice on a calm day.

---

## Why the usual advice backfires

When panic hits, the instinct is to escape. Leave the room. Sit down. Grab some water. Check your pulse. Avoid that train next week.

It works in the moment. That is the problem.

Each escape teaches your brain the same lesson: that was dangerous, and getting out is what saved you. The fear never gets the chance to learn it was a false alarm. Over time, the list of places and situations you avoid quietly grows.

The same goes for "just calm down". Fighting the feeling is still treating it as an emergency.

---

## What actually helps

The good news is that panic responds well to treatment. Cognitive behavioural therapy for panic is recommended by NICE, the body that sets UK health guidance, as a first-line treatment, and it has decades of trial evidence behind it.

A few of its core ideas are things you can start to understand on your own.

**Name it.** Saying "this is a panic attack, it will peak and pass" interrupts the danger story. You are not denying the sensations. You are labelling them correctly.

**Slow the out-breath.** Because fast breathing drives so many of the symptoms, slowing it down helps. Researchers such as Alicia Meuret have shown that training people to breathe more slowly and less deeply can reduce panic symptoms. Try breathing in gently through your nose, then letting the breath out slowly, longer than the in-breath. Don't gulp big lungfuls of air. That makes it worse.

**Stay, if you can.** If you are safe, try not to leave straight away. Let the wave rise and fall where you are. This is how the brain learns the alarm was false.

**Practise the sensations when you are calm.** This one surprises people. In therapy, it is called interoceptive exposure. You deliberately bring on mild versions of the feelings you fear, like a racing heart from running up stairs, or dizziness from spinning in a chair, in a safe and planned way. Your brain learns, slowly, that these sensations are uncomfortable but harmless.

## When to get more help

If panic attacks are frequent, if you are starting to avoid places or plans because of them, or if they are affecting your work or sleep, please speak to your GP. In the UK you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for CBT, without going through your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis or worried about your safety, call NHS 111, or Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.
