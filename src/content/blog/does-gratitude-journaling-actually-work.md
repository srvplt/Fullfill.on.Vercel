You bought the notebook. It had a nice cover.

For the first week you wrote three things every night. Coffee. The sun came out. A good chat with your sister.

By week three you were writing the same three things. Coffee. Weather. Sister. It felt like homework.

Then one night you just didn't. And you felt a bit guilty about not being grateful enough, which is not quite the point.

---

## You are not doing it wrong

Gratitude journaling is one of the most recommended wellbeing habits there is. It is also one of the easiest to drop.

That is not a failure of character. The research suggests the practice is more delicate than the advice makes it sound. Done one way it seems to help. Done another way it can go flat, or even feel worse.

So the honest answer to "does it work?" is: a bit, for some things, if you do it in a particular way. That is less exciting than the headlines, but it is more useful.

---

## What the early studies found

The study that started much of this was published in 2003 by Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

In the first experiment, about 190 students wrote a short list once a week for nine weeks. One group listed things they were grateful for. One listed hassles. One listed neutral events.

The gratitude group rated their lives more positively overall. They felt more optimistic about the week ahead, reported fewer physical complaints, and said they spent more time exercising than the hassles group. In a second experiment with daily lists over about two weeks, the gratitude group had more positive mood and reported offering more emotional support to others.

In 2005 Martin Seligman and colleagues tested a version called "three good things" in the *American Psychologist*. People wrote down three things that went well each day, and why, for one week. Compared with a placebo exercise, they reported higher happiness and fewer depressive symptoms, and the gap was still there six months later. That last part is striking, but it is one study with self-selected volunteers from a website.

## What happened when the evidence grew up

Early studies often compared gratitude with writing about hassles. That makes gratitude look good partly because the comparison makes people feel worse.

Later reviews used fairer comparisons. In 2016, Don Davis and colleagues pooled the trials in the *Journal of Counseling Psychology*. Gratitude exercises did make people feel more grateful. For general wellbeing, the benefit over a matched comparison activity was small. Against other psychologically active exercises, the advantage disappeared. Effects on anxiety were not significant.

In 2021, David Cregg and Jennifer Cheavens at Ohio State looked at 27 studies with 3,675 people in the *Journal of Happiness Studies*. They focused on depression and anxiety. Gratitude exercises helped only slightly more than the control activities. Cheavens summed it up simply: there was a difference, but a small one. Their advice was that people with depression or anxiety should look first to treatments with stronger evidence, such as CBT.

I think that is a fair reading. Gratitude practice is not a treatment. It is a small, cheap, low-risk habit that can nudge how you feel day to day.

## Why it can work at all

Your brain does not weigh good and bad events equally. In a well-known review in 2001, Roy Baumeister and colleagues set out the evidence that negative events tend to grab more attention, linger longer and shape memory more strongly than positive ones of the same size. It is often called the negativity bias.

This is useful when there is a threat around. It is less useful at 10pm, when your mind replays the one awkward moment from a meeting and skips the six things that went fine.

You also get used to good things. A new job, a nicer flat, a partner who makes you tea. They lift you for a while, then become the background. That is hedonic adaptation, and it is part of why "having everything" can feel strangely flat.

Writing down what went well is a way of pushing back against both. It asks your attention to land, briefly and on purpose, on the things it would otherwise skip.

## Why the usual advice fails

The common advice is "write three things you are grateful for every night". There are a few problems with that.

**Doing it too often.** In a small study reported by Sonja Lyubomirsky, Kennon Sheldon and David Schkade in 2005, students who counted their blessings once a week became happier over six weeks. Those who did it three times a week did not. One likely reason is that it became a chore and lost its freshness.

**Repeating the same list.** Writing "my family" every night becomes a ritual, not a noticing. The effect seems to come from actually recalling a moment, not from naming a category.

**Using it to argue with your feelings.** If you are struggling and you tell yourself you should be grateful, the practice turns into another way to feel you are failing. That is the opposite of the goal.

---

## What helps

The evidence points to a few simple adjustments.

**Go less often, go deeper.** Once or twice a week is a reasonable start. Pick one or two things and write a few lines about each, rather than a quick list.

**Be specific.** Not "work", but "Priya stayed late to help me fix the slide and didn't make a thing of it". Specific moments are easier to relive, and they don't go stale.

**Add the why.** The three good things exercise asked people to note why each thing happened. That small step links the good moment to something you or others did, rather than luck.

**Make it about people.** In 2018, Joel Wong and colleagues ran a trial with nearly 300 adults having counselling at a university clinic, published in *Psychotherapy Research*. Some added a gratitude letter writing task to their therapy. Some did expressive writing about stressful experiences. Some had therapy only. The gratitude writers reported better mental health four and twelve weeks later than both other groups. They did not have to send the letters.

**Keep it short at night.** If your mind races in bed, a few minutes of writing can help it settle. I wrote more about why the brain won't switch off at night in [this post](/blog/why-your-brain-wont-switch-off-at-night).

**Stop if it makes you feel worse.** That is useful information, not a failure. Try another approach, or talk to someone.

Our Gratitude pathway is built around this research rather than the notebook-and-three-things version. Short sessions, not every day, with prompts that ask for one specific moment and why it mattered. Some sessions are about other people, because that is where the strongest effects seem to be.

## When to get more help

If your mood has been low for more than a couple of weeks, if you have lost interest in things you used to enjoy, or if anxiety is getting in the way of work, sleep or relationships, please speak to your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for CBT and other psychological support, without seeing your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123, any time of day or night.
