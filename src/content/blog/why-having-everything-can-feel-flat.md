You got the job you wanted. The flat is nicer than the last one. There are people in your life who care about you.

For a few weeks it all felt bright.

Now it is just Tuesday. The commute, the inbox, the same takeaway on Friday.

Nothing is wrong, exactly. It just doesn't feel like much. And you wonder what is wrong with you, since you have everything you said you wanted.

---

## This is how people are built

Psychologists have a name for this. It is called hedonic adaptation, and the idea that we keep running towards happiness without getting any closer is often called the hedonic treadmill.

The most famous study on it was published in 1978 by Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. They interviewed 22 people who had won large prizes on the Illinois State Lottery, 22 people from similar areas who had not, and 29 people who had been paralysed in accidents.

The lottery winners were not significantly happier than the comparison group. They also rated small, everyday pleasures, like talking with a friend, eating breakfast or getting a compliment, as less enjoyable. The big win seemed to make the small things look smaller.

It is a striking result, and it gets quoted a lot. But it was a small study, done once, at one point in time. Later and much larger research gives a more mixed picture.

In 2020, Erik Lindqvist, Robert Östling and David Cesarini surveyed Swedish lottery players 5 to 22 years after their wins, in the *Review of Economic Studies*. Big winners reported higher life satisfaction than matched comparison groups, and the gap had not faded after more than a decade. The effects on day to day happiness and mental health were much smaller.

Money is not meaningless either. In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth collected more than 1.7 million in-the-moment mood reports from 33,391 working adults in the US, via a phone app, and published them in *PNAS*. How people felt rose steadily with income, with no plateau at the often quoted $75,000 a year.

So the treadmill is real, but it is not the whole story. You adapt to a lot. You don't adapt to everything, and you don't adapt to everything at the same speed.

---

## What is actually happening

In 2006, Ed Diener, Richard Lucas and Christie Napa Scollon reviewed the evidence in the *American Psychologist*, in a paper called "Beyond the hedonic treadmill". They argued the old model needed several corrections.

Your baseline is not neutral. For most people the set point is somewhat positive.

People differ. Your set point is partly down to temperament, so some people simply sit higher or lower than others.

Different parts of wellbeing can move separately. How satisfied you are with your life and how good you feel day to day are not the same thing.

And set points can shift. Some changes do last, and people vary a lot in how they adapt. A year later, Lucas summed up evidence from large panel studies in *Current Directions in Psychological Science*. Some events, such as unemployment, divorce, losing a partner and disability, were linked to lasting changes in wellbeing. Adaptation, he wrote, is not inevitable.

So why do the good things fade? In 2012, Kennon Sheldon and Sonja Lyubomirsky proposed a simple model in *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*. They suggested a positive change loses its effect in two ways.

The first is that it stops producing new good moments. The new flat gives you a lift for a while, then it is just where you live. Fewer fresh positive experiences, fewer positive feelings.

The second is that your expectations rise. What felt like a treat becomes the standard. The goalposts move without you noticing, and the same life now feels like the minimum.

---

## Why the usual advice falls flat

The most common response is to look for the next thing. A promotion, a holiday, a new kitchen. It works for a while, then the same thing happens again. That is the treadmill.

The second is to tell yourself to be grateful. You know, logically, that you are lucky. But being told you should feel something rarely makes you feel it. It can just add guilt on top of the flatness.

---

## What seems to help

Sheldon and Lyubomirsky tested their model with 481 students over about 12 weeks, measured three times. The students had all reported a recent positive change in their lives. Two things seemed to slow the fade.

**Variety.** Students who kept having different, varied experiences linked to their change kept more of the benefit. Variety was linked to more positive feelings, and to expectations rising more slowly.

In practice, that might mean using the thing you have in new ways. A different route through the park you moved to be near. Cooking something unfamiliar in the kitchen you wanted. Seeing a friend somewhere you have not been together before. Small changes keep a good thing from becoming background.

**Appreciation.** Students who kept noticing and valuing the change also held on to more of the boost. Appreciation also seemed to slow the creep of rising expectations.

This is not the same as forcing yourself to feel grateful. It is more like paying attention on purpose, now and then, to something that is already good. I looked at what the research says about gratitude practice, and how to do it so it doesn't go stale, in [this post](/blog/does-gratitude-journaling-actually-work).

**Notice the goalposts moving.** You can't stop your expectations rising, but you can spot it. When something that used to please you now feels like the bare minimum, that is adaptation, not a sign your life has got worse.

**Don't rely on one big change.** My reading of this research is that a single big event, even a happy one, is likely to fade. Small, varied good experiences that keep coming seem to give adaptation less to work with.

None of these are dramatic. That is partly the point. The treadmill works on big changes. The way off it seems to be small and ongoing.

## When to get more help

Feeling a bit flat now and then is normal. But flatness can overlap with low mood. If you have lost interest or pleasure in things you used to enjoy for two weeks or more, or you feel low most days, it is worth raising with your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, without seeing your GP first.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
