You leave for work in the dark. You come home in the dark.

The alarm feels heavier than it did in September. Plans you said yes to now feel like chores.

By mid-afternoon you want to eat everything and go to bed at eight.

Nothing has gone wrong, exactly. Everything just feels a bit greyer.

---

## You are not imagining it, but it is not the same for everyone

Around the shortest day, much of the UK gets less than eight hours of daylight, and further north it is closer to seven. Most of those hours are spent indoors. It would be strange if that had no effect at all.

For some people the effect is large enough to count as depression. The NHS describes seasonal affective disorder, or SAD, as depression that comes in winter and goes away in summer. You may be diagnosed with it if the pattern has repeated for two years or more. Symptoms include low mood, losing interest in things you usually enjoy, feeling guilty or worthless, poor concentration, eating more, and sleeping more but still feeling tired.

Here is the part that surprised me when I read the research. For the population as a whole, the winter dip is smaller and less certain than most of us assume.

In 2016, Megan Traffanstedt, Sheila Mehta and Steven LoBello looked at survey data from 34,294 US adults in *Clinical Psychological Science*. They found no link between depressive symptoms and season, latitude or sunlight exposure. In 2020, Simon Øverland and colleagues reviewed 41 studies in *Epidemiology and Psychiatric Sciences*. Only 13 suggested more depression in winter. Their conclusion was that there is some support for seasonal patterns in clinical depression, but not for a general shift to lower mood across the whole population.

That does not mean SAD is made up. Clinics have treated it for decades and the treatments work, as you will see below. It means winter hits some people hard and most people only lightly. Both are real. Knowing which one you are matters, because the right response is different.

## What winter does to your body clock

The most studied explanation is about light and timing.

Your brain runs a roughly 24-hour clock. Morning light is the main signal that keeps it set. It tells your body when to wake up properly, when to release melatonin, when to feel alert and when to wind down.

In winter that signal arrives late and weak. Indoor lighting is far dimmer than daylight, even on a cloudy day. For people who are sensitive to this, the clock can drift later. You are being pulled out of bed at a time your body still treats as night. The result looks a lot like jet lag that lasts for months: heavy mornings, low energy, craving carbohydrates, wanting to sleep more.

Behaviour then does the rest. Dark evenings mean fewer walks, fewer plans and more time on the sofa. Fewer rewarding things happen, so mood drops a little further. That makes going out feel even less appealing. It is a slow loop, and it is easy to miss while you are inside it.

## Why the usual advice falls short

"Just get some fresh air" is not wrong. But it is often given as if a ten-minute stroll at lunch will undo five months of darkness. For someone with real seasonal depression, it usually won't.

The opposite mistake is common too. People hear that SAD exists and assume every grey day in November is a disorder that needs a gadget. Most winter flatness responds to small, boring changes in routine.

There is also a trap in how we cope. Winter makes withdrawal feel sensible. Staying in feels like rest. Sometimes it is. But when staying in becomes the default, it usually feeds the low mood rather than easing it. If you tend to go over the same worries on long dark evenings, I wrote about the difference between helpful and stuck thinking in [rumination vs reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection).

## What the evidence says helps

**Get outside in the morning, every day you can.** Even overcast winter daylight is many times brighter than a typical office. The NHS advice for SAD starts here: get as much natural daylight as you can. Morning light is the most useful for resetting the clock. Walk part of the commute, take a call outside, sit near a window.

**Keep your sleep and wake times steady.** A fixed wake-up time, including weekends, anchors the body clock. It is the same principle behind most good sleep advice. The NHS lists a regular sleep and wake time as part of self-care for SAD.

**Move, ideally outdoors.** Regular exercise is on the NHS self-care list too. A brisk walk in daylight does two jobs at once: light for the clock and activity for mood.

**Plan things you enjoy, on purpose.** In winter, good things stop happening by accident. Put them in the diary: a friend on Thursday, a class on Saturday morning. The NHS also advises staying in touch with friends and family and keeping up your usual activities.

**Light therapy, used carefully.** In 2005, Robert Golden and colleagues published a meta-analysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*. In good-quality trials, bright light for SAD had an effect size of 0.84, and dawn simulation an effect size of 0.73. The authors said this was comparable to many antidepressant trials, while also noting that much of the research was small. The NHS position is more cautious. It says light therapy is not usually available on the NHS because there is not enough evidence to say whether it is effective, though many people find it helps. If you try a lamp, 10,000 lux is standard, it should filter out UV, and the NHS advises speaking to a GP first.

**Talking therapy, which may last longer.** Kelly Rohan and colleagues ran a trial with 177 adults with winter depression. Half had six weeks of CBT adapted for SAD, half had light therapy. Published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2015, the results were similar at the end of treatment: around 47% in each group were in remission on the main clinician-rated scale. The difference showed up later. Two winters on, symptoms had come back in 27.3% of the CBT group compared with 45.6% of the light therapy group (Rohan et al., 2016). Light works while you use it. Skills seem to travel with you.

## When to get more help

See a GP if you think you might have SAD, if low mood is lasting more than a couple of weeks, or if it is getting in the way of work, relationships or looking after yourself. Treatment options include talking therapy and antidepressants, and many people need support each winter to stop symptoms returning.

In England you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies without seeing a GP first. Search "NHS Talking Therapies" and your area.

Fullfill is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call NHS 111 or Samaritans free on 116 123.
